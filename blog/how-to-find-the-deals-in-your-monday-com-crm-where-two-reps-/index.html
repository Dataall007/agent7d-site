How to Find the Deals in Your monday.com CRM Where Two Reps Think They Own the Same Account

📅 October 1, 2026 ✍️ agent7d Team ⏱ 5 min read

Shared ownership sounds mature. Collaborative. Enterprise-grade, even. In practice, it usually means nobody is responsible, both reps are making decisions in isolation, and the client is getting two different versions of reality. This post shows you how to find those situations in your monday.com CRM before they blow up a deal.

The Problem

Duplicate deal ownership is one of the quietest revenue leaks in a monday.com pipeline. It doesn't throw an error. It doesn't trigger an automation. It just sits there — two active deals pointing at the same company, each assigned to a different rep, each moving forward on its own timeline with its own pricing logic and its own promises.

It happens more often than most sales managers realize, and it almost always traces back to one of three causes:

Board duplication during setup. When teams copy a board template or migrate data from a spreadsheet, records get duplicated. Ownership gets assigned manually, and if two people are working from the same source file, you end up with two items for the same account.

Reassignments that weren't completed cleanly. A rep leaves, accounts get redistributed, but someone forgets to archive or close the original deal. Now the new rep has built a fresh item, the old one is still sitting in the pipeline, and the account technically has two active deals.

Territory overlaps. Two enterprise reps, both legitimately targeting a large prospect in a region where their territories blur. Neither is doing anything wrong — but because there's no deduplication process, both end up in your CRM.

The client experience degrades fast once this happens. They're getting calls from two different people, hearing different timelines, different pricing, sometimes different product configurations. From their side, your company looks disorganized. The trust that enterprise deals run on starts to erode — and often you don't find out until they tell you they're going with someone else.

The Solution

The fix isn't complicated, but it does require you to look deliberately. monday.com won't surface this problem automatically. You have to build a filter that does it for you, then establish a policy that prevents it from recurring.

The goal is a view that shows you every company name that appears more than once across your active pipeline, along with who owns each record. Once you can see that, you can resolve conflicts quickly and systematically.

Step by Step

1. Create a filtered view in your CRM board. Start by filtering to show only active deals — exclude closed won, closed lost, and any stage you use for archived records.

2. Group by your Company Name column. This is the critical step. When you group by company, any account with more than one item will visually cluster together. Scroll through and look for groups with two or more items.

3. Add your Owner column to the view. Now you can see, within each cluster, whether the items belong to the same rep or different reps. Different reps on the same account is your red flag.

4. Export or tag the conflicts. Either export to Excel and filter for groups with count > 1 and distinct owners > 1, or create a tag in monday.com like "Ownership Review" and label every duplicate cluster manually.

5. Schedule a resolution conversation. Don't resolve this via Slack. Get the two reps and their manager in a room (or a call) and work through each conflict with the questions below.

Before deciding who owns the account, ask:

  • Which rep made first contact, and when?
  • Which rep has the stronger relationship with the economic buyer?
  • What commitments have already been made to the client by each rep?
  • Is there a territory policy that should determine this?

The answer to ownership should follow your rules, not whoever argues loudest.

Real Example

A logistics company with a twelve-person sales team ran a standard quarterly pipeline review. Nothing looked unusual until their head of sales noticed a familiar company name appearing twice in the enterprise segment — once under their East Coast rep, once under their West Coast rep.

Both reps had been actively working the account for six weeks. Both had submitted pricing. The East Coast rep had offered a volume discount. The West Coast rep, unaware of that, had held firm on standard rates. The prospect had received two formal proposals with a $40,000 annual difference between them.

The prospect didn't ask for clarification. They sent a single email citing "mixed messages and unclear internal communication" as the reason they were pausing the evaluation. The deal was lost.

When the sales manager traced the history, the duplication had started with a territory reassignment three months earlier. The original item was never archived. The new rep created a fresh one. Nobody noticed because the pipeline view was filtered by owner — which meant neither rep ever saw the other's item.

The total value of that deal was $180,000. The fix would have taken about twenty minutes.

How to Get Started

Start with the group-by-company audit described above. If your board has clean, consistent company name formatting, this will surface conflicts in under an hour. If your data is messy — inconsistent capitalization, abbreviations, subsidiaries listed under different names — you'll need to do some cleanup first before the grouping becomes reliable.

Once you've resolved existing conflicts, implement a lightweight policy: any new deal that comes in must be checked against the existing pipeline before it's created. A simple "search before you create" norm, enforced in your team's onboarding, eliminates most future duplication.

For shared ownership situations that are genuinely legitimate — co-selling arrangements, enterprise accounts with multiple divisions — create a structured item in monday.com where one person is designated Lead Owner and the other is a Supporting Rep. Use separate columns, not the same Owner field, so it's unambiguous who holds primary accountability.

Ownership ambiguity is a policy problem before it's a CRM problem. Fix the policy, and the CRM reflects it.
