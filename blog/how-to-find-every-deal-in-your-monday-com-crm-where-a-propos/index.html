How to Find Every Deal in Your monday.com CRM Where a Proposal Was Sent But No Follow-Up Was Ever Logged

📅 November 1, 2026 ✍️ agent7d Team ⏱ 5 min read

Most proposals are forgotten within 72 hours. Not by the prospect — by the rep who sent them.

That's the uncomfortable truth behind one of the most common causes of deal decay in monday.com pipelines. A proposal goes out, the rep moves on to the next thing, and the deal sits untouched — technically alive, practically dead. Nobody chased. Nobody called. The prospect assumed you weren't that interested, and quietly moved on.

If you've never audited your pipeline specifically for this pattern, there's a reasonable chance it's happening right now at a scale that would genuinely surprise you.

The Problem

The gap between "Proposal Sent" and "Follow-Up Logged" is where revenue goes to disappear.

Research consistently shows that deals receiving a follow-up within 48 hours of a proposal close at significantly higher rates than those that don't. That window matters enormously — it's when the proposal is freshest, when questions are forming in the prospect's mind, and when your presence signals confidence rather than desperation.

When that follow-up doesn't happen, the deal doesn't necessarily die immediately. It lingers. It stays in your pipeline, inflating your forecast, misleading your revenue projections, and quietly decaying. By the time anyone notices it's cold, weeks have passed and re-engagement is awkward at best.

The problem is compounded in monday.com when teams haven't structured their boards to make this gap visible. If there's no "Proposal Sent" stage, no activity logging discipline, and no view that surfaces stale records, the whole thing stays invisible.

The Solution

Fix the visibility first. The follow-up behaviour follows.

You need two things working together in your monday.com CRM: a clearly defined Proposal Sent status in your deal stage column, and a consistent practice of logging follow-up activity — whether that's a call, an email, or a meeting — as a dated action on each record.

Monday.com's Activity Log column or a dedicated Last Activity Date date column gives you the timestamp you need. Pair that with your deal stage and your proposal send date (which should be its own column, not buried in a note), and you have everything required to filter for exactly the records where follow-up never happened.

This isn't about adding complexity. It's about making the silence visible so it can't be ignored.

Step by Step

Here's how to run this audit manually inside monday.com:

  • Open your CRM board and ensure you have three columns in place: Deal Stage, Proposal Sent Date, and Last Activity Date. If Last Activity Date doesn't exist, add it and make it a required field going forward.
  • Filter by stage. Set your filter to show only deals where the Deal Stage is "Proposal Sent" or any stage beyond it (e.g., Negotiation, Pending Decision).
  • Add a formula or sort by date gap. Sort by Last Activity Date ascending. You're looking for records where the Last Activity Date is either blank or predates the Proposal Sent Date by more than five days. These are your silent deals.
  • Export the list. Use monday.com's export function to pull this into a working spreadsheet. Add a column to note deal value and assigned owner.
  • Flag each record. Tag them in monday.com with a label like "Needs Triage" so they're visible in dashboards and nothing slips back into the noise.

    • This process works. It's just slow, and it requires your data to be clean enough to trust. If your pipeline has inconsistent stage naming, missing dates, or unowned records, the manual route gets messy fast.

    Real Example

    A professional services firm running their pipeline on monday.com came to us after noticing their close rate had dropped without any obvious cause. We walked them through a manual version of this audit.

    The result: 34 proposals sent in the previous quarter with zero logged follow-up activity. Not delayed follow-up — none at all. Those 34 deals represented over £180,000 in open pipeline that had effectively been abandoned. The proposals went out, the reps moved on, and nobody noticed until we looked.

    Some of those deals were recoverable. Several prospects responded positively to re-engagement emails sent within days of the audit. Two converted within the following month. But the firm also had to reckon with the coaching implications — this wasn't a one-rep problem, it was a systemic gap in how follow-up accountability was structured.
