When Two Reps Define 'Proposal Sent' Differently, Who's Right?

📅 November 19, 2026 ✍️ agent7d Team ⏱ 5 min read

Both of them. That's the problem.

Stage names in your monday.com pipeline feel like shared language. Everyone on the team uses the same board, clicks the same status column, moves deals through the same stages. It looks like a system. But if two reps are making different judgment calls about what "Proposal Sent" actually means, you don't have a system — you have a leaderboard built on different rules.

And you won't see it until a deal you thought was close goes cold, or a forecast call turns into an uncomfortable conversation.

The Problem

Stage names are labels. Without exit criteria — a specific condition that must be true before a deal moves forward — each rep defines the stage themselves, based on instinct, habit, or how they were trained at their last job.

Take "Proposal Sent." One rep moves a deal there the moment she shares a deck over email. Another waits until he's sent a formal quote, the prospect has acknowledged receipt, and both parties have signed an NDA. Same stage name. Completely different commercial reality.

Now multiply that across five reps, eight stages, and 60 open deals. Your pipeline report is aggregating apples and aircraft carriers.

The forecast distortion is subtle at first. Close dates get pushed. Deals sit in mid-stages longer than expected. Someone says the pipeline is "lumpy." What they're describing is stage-consistency failure — they just don't have a name for it yet.

The Solution

Exit criteria. One line per stage, written in plain language, describing the minimum condition that must be true before a deal advances.

Not a philosophy. A fact. Something you can verify by looking at the deal record.

"Proposal Sent: A formal quote has been emailed and the prospect has confirmed a decision date." That's it. If the rep can't confirm both things are true, the deal stays in the previous stage.

Exit criteria turn subjective judgment into a binary check. They also make coaching easier — instead of debating whether a deal is "real," you're just asking whether a specific condition was met.

Step by Step

Here's how to fix this without calling a team training day:

  • Pull your current stage list from monday.com. Write it out on a doc — just the names, nothing else.
  • For each stage, write one exit criterion. Focus on what the prospect has done, not what the rep has done. "Rep sent proposal" is rep activity. "Prospect confirmed budget and decision date" is a commercial signal.
  • Add the criterion as a column description or sticky note in monday.com. The board is where the work happens — put the rule where people can see it.
  • Audit existing deals against the new criteria. Yes, this is uncomfortable. Some deals will drop back a stage. That's the point.
  • Pick one stage at a time. Start with the stage where deals pile up longest. Fix that one, let it settle, then move to the next.

    A sales manager notices that "Proposal Sent" is by far the most crowded stage in her pipeline — more than twice as many deals as any other stage, and they've been sitting there for weeks. She runs the free CRM Audit Tool on her monday.com board.

    The audit didn't diagnose the personality difference. It surfaced the data pattern that made her go look. That's the job.

    How to Get Started

    Start with your own board. Look at which stage has the most deals. Ask yourself whether you'd bet your forecast number on every deal in that stage being accurately placed. If you hesitate, you have a consistency problem worth investigating.

    If you want to move faster, the CRM Audit Tool does the pattern-matching for you — it flags stage distribution skew, field completeness gaps by rep, and estimates how much revenue is sitting in deals that have gone quiet. You can then dig into the specific conversations that the data points to.

    The exit criteria work is yours to do either way. The audit just tells you where to start.
