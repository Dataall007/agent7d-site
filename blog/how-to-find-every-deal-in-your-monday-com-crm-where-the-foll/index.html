How to Find Every Deal in Your monday.com CRM Where the Follow-Up Was Logged But the Stage Never Moved — and What That Pattern Reveals About Your Pipeline

📅 November 10, 2026 ✍️ agent7d Team ⏱ 5 min read

There's a specific kind of pipeline problem that's hard to catch because it doesn't look like a problem. The deals are being touched. Updates are being logged. Reps are making calls and sending emails and writing notes. On the surface, everything appears to be moving.

It isn't.

When activity is being logged but stages aren't advancing, you're looking at stagnation dressed up as effort. And in most monday.com pipelines, this pattern is hiding a significant chunk of forecasted revenue that was never actually going to close.

---

The Problem

Logged activity creates a false sense of pipeline health. When a sales manager reviews a board and sees recent updates, check-in notes, and call logs, the natural assumption is that the deal is alive and progressing. CRM hygiene looks good. The rep looks engaged. The deal stays in the forecast.

But there's a critical distinction between activity and advancement. A rep can send a follow-up email every Tuesday for six weeks without the deal ever moving from "Proposal Sent" to "Negotiation." If the stage column isn't changing, the deal isn't progressing — regardless of how much is being logged in the updates feed.

This is one of the most dangerous pipeline illusions in monday.com because it actively misleads forecasting. These deals look warm. They occupy space in your pipeline. They inflate your weighted revenue numbers. And they give reps a reason to keep them on the board without being pushed to either advance or disqualify them.

The technical term for this pattern is monday.com deals with no stage movement. The practical term is revenue leaking quietly while everyone stays busy.

---

The Solution

The fix starts with visibility. You need to isolate every deal where updates, calls, or emails have been logged — but where the pipeline stage column hasn't changed in 30, 60, or 90 days. Once you can see these deals together, the patterns become obvious fast.

This isn't about blaming reps for activity. It's about distinguishing genuine nurture from deals that are quietly dying — and doing something about them before they fall off the edge of your forecast.

---

Step by Step

Here's how to surface these deals directly inside your monday.com board:

1. Filter by Stage + Last Updated

Use the Filter function on your pipeline board to show deals in mid-funnel stages — anything from "Proposal Sent" through "Negotiation." Then sort by Last Updated descending. You're looking for deals that show recent update activity but have been sitting in the same stage for a long time.

2. Add a Stage Change Date Column

If you haven't already, add a Date column specifically for tracking when the stage last changed — not when the item was last updated. These are different things. An update timestamp refreshes whenever anyone types a note. A stage-change date only moves when the stage actually moves.

3. Use the Activity Log to Cross-Reference

Open individual deal records and check the update thread against the stage history. If you see six weeks of weekly check-ins but zero stage movement, that deal needs immediate attention.

4. Set a 30/60/90 Day Threshold

Create a filter view that flags any deal where the stage hasn't changed in more than 30 days. Deals crossing 60 or 90 days with no movement should be treated as critical — either re-engage with a clear next step or disqualify.

5. Review the Pattern Across the Board

One stuck deal is a rep issue. Ten stuck deals is a process issue. Look at which stages are accumulating the most stationary deals — that's where your pipeline is breaking down.

---

Real Example

A SaaS sales team using monday.com ran a manual audit of their pipeline after a missed quarterly target. What they found: 14 deals sitting in "Proposal Sent" where reps had logged weekly check-in calls for two months straight.

The reps weren't slacking — they were following up consistently. But the deals weren't moving because no one was asking the hard qualifying questions: Is there a real decision timeline? Is there budget? Is there an internal champion?

Collectively, those 14 deals represented $180,000 in forecasted revenue. None of them closed that quarter. Most were quietly disqualified or abandoned in the following weeks. The activity logs had made them look healthy right up until the end.

The cost wasn't just the lost revenue — it was the opportunity cost of deals that could have been in the pipeline instead.
